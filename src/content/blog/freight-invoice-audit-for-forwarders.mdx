Freight invoice audit for forwarders means checking every line on a carrier, co-loader, or agent invoice against the buy rate you were quoted at booking and the tariff notice that governs each surcharge, then reconciling the result against what you re-billed the customer. That is a different job from the shipper-side audit most software and most guides describe, where the reference document is a long-term carrier contract.

The difference matters more in 2026 than it did two years ago. Surcharge volatility was already the top external risk for 63% of forwarders going into the year, according to a forwarder survey ([CargoForwarder Global, 21 December 2025](https://cargoforwarder.eu/2025/12/21/forwarders-expect-margins-to-tighten-further-in-2026/)). Since then carriers have stacked war-risk, canal, drought, and emergency cost-recovery lines on top of base rates, and a quote issued on a Monday can be invoiced against a different surcharge schedule by the time the box sails. This guide covers the current surcharge stack, the duty refund surface that appeared this year, a line-item audit workflow, and a comparison of the three ways to run it. ## Why Shipper-Side Freight Audit Does Not Fit a Forwarder

Shipper-side freight audit compares a carrier invoice against the shipper's own contract with that carrier and pays or disputes the difference. Cass, Trax, Intelligent Audit, and similar platforms do this well for parcel and truckload shippers with stable contracts and high invoice counts. A forwarder's reference point is different, and that is why the tools do not transfer cleanly.

On the buy side, the invoice comes from a carrier, a co-loader, or a destination agent, and the price it should match is the buy rate on that specific booking: a spot quote, a named-account contract, or a co-loader's weekly rate sheet. Meanwhile the forwarder has often already invoiced the customer before the carrier bill arrived. Auditing means closing both loops: was the buy invoice right, and did the sell invoice pass through the right charges at the right margin?

The top-ranking guides skip this. The "Best Freight Audit Software" roundup explicitly leaves out contract terms, data portability, migration, and dispute handling by carrier type ([Supply Chain Desk, 22 August 2026](https://www.supplychaindesk.com/blog/best-freight-audit-software/)). Intelligent Audit's own newsletter raised the in-house versus outsourced question and noted that a reliable audit "requires connected data, financial controls, carrier integrations, ongoing maintenance" ([Intelligent Audit, 3 September 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/september-3-2026-intelligent-audit-3eehf)). For a forwarder, those integrations point at CargoWise or SAP TM job files, not a shipper's ERP.

| Emergency operating cost recovery (EOCR) | Catch-all line that needs a published notice to validate | [GoFreight](https://gofreight.com/blog/maritime-disruption) (vendor blog, qualitative) |
| EU ETS surcharge | Emissions cost pass-through on EU-touching legs; GoFreight recommends explicit fuel, ETS, and PSS clauses in customer quotes | [GoFreight, H2 2026 note](https://gofreight.com/blog/h2-2026-ocean-freight-forecast) (vendor blog, clause recommendation only) |
| Terminal handling | Port-specific tariffs, often billed by a different party from the ocean carrier | Terminal tariffs |
| Currency adjustment factor | Applied on the carrier's index date, which may differ from your quote date | Carrier tariffs |

Two things stand out. First, several of these lines were introduced or revised inside a normal quote validity window. A 14-day quote issued in mid-August on a Brazil or Gulf lane could be invoiced against a canal or drought surcharge that did not exist, or was a different amount, when the quote went out. That drift is an inference from index moves rather than forwarder-reported validity data, so treat the timing risk as real and the magnitude as unmeasured.

Second, a surcharge is not wrong because it is new. It is wrong if it was not in the buy rate you were quoted, if it was applied before its effective date, if it hit the wrong equipment type, or if it was run off the wrong index. Manual review that compares invoice total to quoted total will accept a $200 EOCR line as roughly right because the base rate came in $150 under. Line-item review flags both.

### How mid-quote surcharges turn into invoice variance

The sequence is predictable. The pricing desk quotes a buy rate from a rate sheet or carrier portal. Between booking confirmation and sailing, the carrier publishes a surcharge notice. The invoice lands weeks later carrying the new line, nobody on the ops team saw the notice, and the sell invoice went out at the original number. Whether you can pass it through depends on the quote's terms, which is why [rate-sheet intelligence](/solutions/rate-sheet-intelligence/) matters upstream of audit: if surcharge validity and effective dates are captured at quote time, the audit rule already knows what the invoice is allowed to contain.

## Duty: The Second Audit Surface in 2026

Forwarders and brokers that paid duties on behalf of clients now have refund money to reconcile, and that reconciliation is invoice audit work by another name. Two windows are open.

**IEEPA refunds via CAPE.** After the Supreme Court held on 20 February 2026 that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs, the Court of International Trade ordered refund processing on 4 March and CBP built the CAPE system (Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries). CBP has reported approximately $128.68 billion in potential and certified refunds accepted for processing ([Greenberg Traurig, May 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/5/us-tariff-update-section-122-duties-found-unauthorized-by-law-ieepa-refunds-under-way); [BDO](https://www.bdo.com/insights/tax/ieepa-tariff-refunds-frequently-asked-questions); [NRF](https://nrf.com/blog/ieepa-tariff-refunds-are-moving-forward)). Every refund maps to an entry, and every entry maps to a client invoice on which the duty was disbursed. If your sell invoice charged the client duty plus a disbursement fee, the refund has to be matched to that invoice, netted, and credited.

**Section 301 refunds via PSC.** The Section 301 China conforming amendment changed 10-digit HTSUS reporting categories for four exclusions effective 1 July 2026. CBP issued [CSMS #69990649](https://content.govdelivery.com/bulletins/gd/USDHSCBP-42bf8f9) on 22 September, ACE acceptance went live at noon on 23 September, and importers with qualifying entries should file a post-summary correction (PSC) on or after that date to obtain refunds ([GHY, September 2026](https://www.ghy.com/trade-compliance/section-301-china-conforming-amendment)).

The audit surface has the same shape as freight: a paid amount, a reference document (the entry summary), a rule with an effective date, and a variance to claim. Refund filing is a recurring workload for brokers in 2026 that did not exist in 2025, and it is entry-data reconciliation by nature. If your pipeline already captures invoices at line level, adding entry summaries is a schema change, not a new project.

## How to Audit Carrier Invoices Against Your Buy Rates

A forwarder-side audit runs six steps: capture every invoice at line-item level, match it to the booking and quote record, validate each surcharge against a contract or tariff notice with an effective date, flag variance with the specific line, route the dispute, and reconcile refunds and credits on both the buy and sell side.

**1. Capture at line-item level.** Total-level capture is where most manual audits stop and most overcharges survive. Every charge line, with its description, basis (per container, per kilo, per shipment), currency, and quantity, has to become structured data. FreightMynd's [smart invoice processing](/solutions/smart-invoice-processing/) pipeline does this across carrier PDFs, co-loader spreadsheets, and agent emails without per-supplier templates, at a 95% first-pass match rate to bookings in production.

**2. Match to the booking and quote.** The reference is the booking record and the quote behind it: buy rate, validity dates, equipment type, and which surcharges were included or excluded. The mechanics are covered in our post on [3-way invoice matching for freight forwarders](/blog/3-way-invoice-matching-freight-forwarders/).

**3. Validate each surcharge against its notice.** Every surcharge line gets checked against a rule with an effective date, an amount or formula, an applicable lane, and an applicable equipment type, taken from your carrier contract, the carrier's published notice, or the co-loader's rate sheet.

| Surcharge | Typical trigger | Validate against | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| War risk | Routing through a declared risk zone | Carrier notice: effective date, per-TEU and per-reefer amounts | Applied to a routing that avoided the zone; reefer rate on a dry box |
| Panama Canal | Canal transit or draft restrictions in force | Carrier notice with effective month | Charged on a Suez or Cape routing; charged before the notice took effect |
| Drought (Amazon, Rhine) | River leg on the routing | Carrier or feeder notice | Per shipment where the notice says per container; kept after the notice lapsed |
| Peak season (PSS) | Carrier-declared peak window | Notice with start and end date | Billed after the end date; billed on a lane with no PSS declared |
| EOCR | Carrier-declared emergency | Notice | Duplicated with a diversion or emergency bunker line for the same cost |
| Fuel / BAF | Monthly or quarterly index | Index value for the invoice period, unless your quote fixed it | Wrong month's index; fixed in the quote but floated on the invoice |
| EU ETS | EU port call on the routing | Carrier's published ETS factor for the period | Charged on a non-EU leg; double-counted with a bunker line |

**4. Flag variance with the specific line.** An exception should name the line, the invoiced value, the expected value, the rule, and the notice or contract clause behind the rule. That package is what a dispute needs. FreightMynd's revenue recovery system generates it automatically and, across carrier invoices in production, detects an average overcharge rate of 4.2%, with 2-5% of total freight spend identified as recoverable.

**5. Route to dispute.** A carrier dispute goes through the carrier's claims process with the notice attached. A co-loader dispute is usually a conversation and a credit note. An agent dispute may net against your own payable to them. Route by counterparty type.

**6. Reconcile on both sides.** Once a credit or refund arrives, match it to the buy invoice, then check whether the sell invoice to your customer needs a credit as well. The same applies to CAPE and PSC duty refunds. Shipper-side tools skip this step because a shipper has no sell side.

## Shipper-Side SaaS vs Outsourced Audit vs a Custom Pipeline

There are three realistic ways to run this audit, and each fits a different forwarder. The comparison comes down to what the reference document is and who owns the data afterwards.

| | Shipper-side audit SaaS (Cass, Trax, Intelligent Audit) | Outsourced audit firm | Custom pipeline on your own data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reference document | The shipper's carrier contract | Whatever you hand them, usually contracts | Your quote and booking record per shipment, plus tariff notices |
| Strongest on | Parcel and domestic truckload at volume | Historical overcharge recovery on contingency | Ocean and air surcharge validation, co-loader and agent invoices, sell-side reconciliation |
| Sell-side reconciliation | None; shippers have no sell side | Rarely | Built in; the job file is the anchor |
| Duty refunds (CAPE, PSC) | Not in scope | Sometimes, as a separate service | Same pipeline, entry summaries as a second document type |
| Data portability | Pricing "not published" (Cass) or "scales with invoice volume" (APT); exit terms not addressed in the ranking guides ([Supply Chain Desk, 22 August 2026](https://www.supplychaindesk.com/blog/best-freight-audit-software/)) | Your data lives in their process | You own the extraction, rules, and history in your TMS |
| Fits when | Large shipper, or a forwarder with a shipper-like domestic book | Low volume, one-off recovery, no appetite to run it | Ocean and air heavy, many suppliers, CargoWise or SAP TM job files |

Shipper-side platforms are good products for the problem they were built for. If your book is largely domestic truckload on a few carrier contracts, they will do the job. The mismatch shows up the moment your reference is a per-booking buy rate from a co-loader, or when a credit note has to flow through to the customer invoice. Outsourced audit is worth considering for a one-time historical sweep, especially on duty refunds where a contingency fee is easier to justify than building, and a poor fit for ongoing operations because the audit lives outside your job file.

A custom pipeline is the most work up front and the only option that puts the quote, the buy invoice, the sell invoice, the notice, and the refund in one record. Intelligent Audit's list of what a reliable audit needs (connected data, financial controls, carrier integrations, ongoing maintenance) is accurate, and it is also scope you can own. ## Where to Start

Start with one supplier type and one surcharge family. Pull the last 90 days of invoices from your highest-volume co-loader or carrier, capture them at line level, and check every war-risk, canal, drought, and PSS line against the notice that governs it. The pattern will show quickly, and it will tell you whether the leak is timing (applied before the effective date), scope (wrong lane or equipment), or pass-through (buy side correct, sell side never updated). Then check whether quote validity, included surcharges, and effective dates already live in your TMS. If they live in email threads and rate-sheet PDFs, that capture is the first build.
